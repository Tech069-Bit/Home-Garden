Grafting techniques
Common methods, when to use them, and how

Whip and tongue

Similar-thickness stems
Best for: Scion and rootstock of about the same pencil-thick diameter — apples, pears, grapes.
When: Late winter to early spring, while both are still dormant.
How: Make one matching 3–5cm diagonal slice on each piece, cut a small "tongue" partway down each face, interlock them so the green cambium touches on at least one side, then wrap tightly with grafting tape.

Cleft graft

Thick rootstock, thin scion
Best for: Topworking an older, thicker branch or trunk with a new variety.
When: Late winter, just before bud break.
How: Saw the rootstock flat, split the stub 4–5cm deep, wedge it open, and insert one or two wedge-cut scions so their cambium lines up with the rootstock's edge. Seal every cut surface with grafting wax.

Bud grafting (T-bud / chip bud)

One bud only
Best for: Roses, citrus, and fruit trees — one scion stick can propagate many plants this way.
When: Late summer, when the rootstock's bark "slips" easily away from the wood.
How: Cut a T (or a shield-shaped chip) in the rootstock bark, slide in a bud shield sliced from the scion, then wrap firmly, leaving just the bud exposed. Cut the rootstock's top back only once the bud has taken, the next season.

Side veneer graft

Indoors, any season
Best for: Ornamentals and potted conifers/succulents, since it doesn't need dormancy.
When: Any time indoors or in a greenhouse.
How: Make a shallow, downward-angled cut into the side of the rootstock without removing its top, shape a matching wedge on the scion's base, fit them together and tie. Remove the rootstock's top growth only after the union has clearly taken.

Approach graft

Two living, rooted plants
Best for: Difficult-to-root or especially valuable plants, since both halves stay alive and rooted throughout.
When: Any time during active growth, ideally spring–summer.
How: Sit both potted plants side by side, shave a matching sliver of bark off each stem where they touch, bind the wounds together, and leave both plants rooted for 6–8 weeks until the union heals — then sever the scion below the graft and the rootstock above it.
General rules: always use a clean, sharp blade; line up the green cambium layer just under the bark on both pieces, not just the bark itself; and wrap or seal every cut so it doesn't dry out.

Fertiliser & pest-care reference
Basic homemade and market options, by purpose — what, when, how
Growth (leafy, vegetative)

Compost tea

Homemade
What: Gentle nitrogen and micronutrients from decomposed kitchen/garden waste — builds leafy growth.
When: Every 2–3 weeks through spring and summer, while the plant is actively growing.
How: Steep a handful of finished compost in water for 3–5 days, strain, dilute 1:10 with water, and pour at the soil around the base.

Used tea leaves / coffee grounds

Homemade
What: Mild nitrogen boost, and improves soil texture over time.
When: Weekly, in small amounts.
How: Let leaves/grounds dry, sprinkle a thin layer around the base and work lightly into the topsoil — never pile it against the stem.

Urea (46-0-0)

Market
What: Fast-acting, concentrated nitrogen for quick leafy growth.
When: Early in the growing season only; stop once flowering begins.
How: Dissolve about 1g per litre of water and water it into the soil, not onto the leaves. Easy to overdo — too much causes leaf burn.

Balanced NPK (e.g. 19-19-19)

Market
What: An even, all-round feed for general growth.
When: Once a month during active growth.
How: Mix 1–2g per litre of water as a soil drench, and water the plant well afterwards.
Nutrition (flowering & fruiting)

Banana peel tea

Homemade
What: Potassium boost that supports flowering and fruit development.
When: Every 2 weeks once buds start forming.
How: Soak chopped peels in water for 2–3 days, strain, dilute 1:5, and pour at the base.

Wood ash

Homemade
What: Potassium and trace minerals; also raises soil pH slightly.
When: Sparingly, before flowering.
How: Dust a thin layer over the topsoil and work it in lightly. Skip it for acid-loving plants, and don't repeat too often.

DAP (18-46-0)

Market
What: Phosphorus-heavy feed for root and flower development.
When: As flowering or fruiting begins.
How: Dissolve about 1g per litre, water into the soil, and keep it off the foliage.

Bloom booster (e.g. 13-40-13)

Market
What: Phosphorus/potassium blend that promotes flowering and fruit set.
When: From bud formation through fruiting, roughly every 2 weeks.
How: Follow the label dose as a soil drench — rates vary more between brands than homemade recipes do.
Pest control

Neem oil spray

Homemade
What: General insect repellent with mild fungal control.
When: Weekly as prevention, or right away at the first sign of pests — spray early morning or evening.
How: Mix ~5ml neem oil with a few drops of liquid soap per litre of water, spray both sides of the leaves, and avoid direct sun straight after spraying.

Garlic-chilli spray

Homemade
What: Repels aphids, mites, and other soft-bodied pests.
When: Every 4–5 days once you spot an infestation.
How: Blend garlic, chilli, and water, steep overnight, strain well, and spray directly on the affected foliage.

Neem-based pesticide

Market
What: Broad-spectrum organic pest control, stronger and more consistent than homemade neem spray.
When: Preventively every 2 weeks, or curatively once pests appear. Reapply after rain.
How: Dilute per the product label and spray during cooler hours (morning/evening) to protect pollinators.

Systemic insecticide (e.g. imidacloprid)

Market
What: Controls sucking pests like aphids and whiteflies from inside the plant's tissue.
When: Only once pests are actually visible — not as routine prevention.
How: Follow the label dose exactly, applied as a soil drench or spray.
Highly toxic to bees and other pollinators — never spray open flowers, and avoid use shortly before harvesting edible produce.
